Illinois' Affidavit of Merit (735 ILCS 5/2-622): a practical guide for medical malpractice filings

Published by ConveneMD · Last updated October 2026 · General information, not legal advice.

Every medical malpractice case filed in Illinois runs through the same gate: Section 2-622 of the Code of Civil Procedure. Before a case can proceed, the plaintiff must file an affidavit certifying that a qualified health professional has reviewed the facts and concluded, in a written report, that there is a reasonable and meritorious cause for filing. Get the review wrong — wrong specialty, thin report, blown deadline — and a meritorious case can be dismissed before it starts. This guide covers what the statute actually requires, who qualifies to review, and where filings most often fail.

What 2-622 requires

For each defendant named in a medical malpractice action, the plaintiff's attorney (or the plaintiff, if unrepresented) must file an affidavit declaring that they have consulted a health professional who meets the statute's qualifications, that the reviewer determined in a written report — after reviewing the relevant medical records and facts — that a reasonable and meritorious cause exists, and that the attorney has concluded, on the basis of that review, that there is a meritorious basis for filing. A copy of the reviewing professional's written report must be attached to the affidavit and complaint, with the reviewer's identity subject to the disclosure rules that follow early in the case.

Who qualifies as the reviewing physician

The statute is specific, and courts enforce it. The reviewing health professional must:

  • Be knowledgeable in the relevant issues involved in the case and familiar with the applicable standard of care;
  • Practice (or have practiced) in the same area of health care at issue, or teach in that area — the statute looks to recent experience, within the six years before the review;
  • Hold the appropriate credentials for the defendant's specialty — in practice, the reviewer should be in the same or a closely similar specialty as the treating provider whose care is challenged.

A neurosurgeon's care reviewed by a family physician, or a report from a reviewer who left clinical practice a decade ago, invites a motion to strike — and Illinois courts grant them. Matching the reviewer's specialty and active-practice status to the defendant's is not a formality; it is the substance of the requirement.

The clock

The affidavit and report are due with the complaint. Where the statute of limitations is close to expiring, 2-622 allows a plaintiff to file with an affidavit explaining that the review could not be completed in time, which opens a statutory window (commonly 90 days) to complete the review and file the certificate. Treat that window as unforgiving: it is the single most common mechanical failure point in Illinois med-mal filings, and it is entirely a function of how fast a qualified reviewer can be found and complete the review.

What a strong merit review contains

  • A complete record chronology — the reviewer works from the full chart, not excerpts chosen to support a theory;
  • A standard-of-care analysis tied to the defendant's specialty and the circumstances at the time of treatment;
  • A causation opinion connecting the deviation to the injury, stated with reasonable medical certainty;
  • Conclusions the reviewer will stand behind — the report should read like the opening of testimony, because functionally it is.

Where filings fail

  • Wrong-specialty reviewer — the report is struck and the case is exposed to dismissal;
  • Conclusory reports — a one-paragraph endorsement without analysis does not satisfy the statute's purpose;
  • Deadline mechanics — the review simply cannot be sourced fast enough inside the limitations window;
  • Retired or non-practicing reviewers — recent-practice requirements are checked, by courts and by defense counsel.

Defense practices meet the mirror-image problem: a defensibility review early enough to matter requires the same specialty-matched, actively practicing physician, on the same unforgiving timeline.
