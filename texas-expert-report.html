Texas' Chapter 74 expert report (§74.351): a practical guide for medical malpractice cases

Published by ConveneMD · Last updated October 2026 · General information, not legal advice.

Every Texas health care liability claim runs through the same gate: Chapter 74 of the Civil Practice and Remedies Code, and specifically §74.351. Within 120 days after each defendant answers, the claimant must serve an expert report — with the expert's CV — that satisfies the statute's content and qualification rules. Miss the deadline, or serve a report the court finds deficient, and the claim against that defendant is dismissed with prejudice, with the defendant's attorney's fees and costs charged to the claimant. This guide covers what the statute actually requires, who qualifies to write the report, and where Texas cases most often fail.

What §74.351 requires

For each physician or health care provider against whom a claim is asserted, the claimant must serve one or more expert reports, together with the curriculum vitae of each expert listed, not later than the 120th day after the date that defendant's original answer is filed. (A later date applies in the narrow circumstances of §74.353, and the parties may extend the deadline by written agreement.) The report must provide a fair summary of the expert's opinions regarding:

  • The applicable standards of care for the condition and circumstances at issue;
  • The manner in which the care rendered failed to meet those standards; and
  • The causal relationship between that failure and the injury, harm, or damages claimed.

Different experts may cover different defendants or different issues — liability and causation, for example (§74.351(i)) — but every defendant and every element must be covered. A defendant who wants to challenge the report's sufficiency must file objections by the later of 21 days after the report is served or 21 days after its answer was filed; miss that window and the objections are waived. The report itself is not evidence: it is not admissible, may not be used in a deposition or at trial, and may not be referred to by any party during the case (§74.351(k)).

Who qualifies as the expert

For an opinion that a physician departed from accepted standards of medical care, §74.401 limits the role to a physician who:

  • Is practicing medicine when the testimony is given, or was practicing medicine when the claim arose;
  • Has knowledge of the accepted standards of medical care for the diagnosis, care, or treatment of the illness, injury, or condition involved in the claim; and
  • Is qualified on the basis of training or experience to offer an expert opinion regarding those standards.

In applying those criteria, courts look at whether the expert is board certified or has other substantial training or experience in an area of medical practice relevant to the claim, and whether the expert is actively practicing medicine in rendering services relevant to the claim. The causation opinion must come from a physician as well. In practice, the expert who survives a §74.351 challenge is in the same or a closely related specialty and actively treating the same condition — a mismatch on specialty, active practice, or the condition at issue is the first thing a challenge attacks.

The clocks around the report

  • Before suit (§74.051): written notice of the claim must be given by certified mail, return receipt requested, to each physician or provider at least 60 days before filing, accompanied by the §74.052 authorization form for release of health information; proper notice tolls the statute of limitations for 75 days.
  • One clock per defendant: each defendant's original answer starts its own 120-day report deadline, so multi-defendant cases run on staggered clocks.
  • One cure, 30 days: if a timely report is found deficient, the court may grant a single 30-day extension to cure the deficiency (§74.351(c)). There is no second cure.
  • Discovery waits: until the report and CV are served, discovery is stayed apart from narrow exceptions, such as obtaining the claimant's medical records.
  • The penalty: if no timely report is served, then on the defendant's motion the court must award that defendant its reasonable attorney's fees and court costs and dismiss the claim with prejudice to refiling (§74.351(b)).

What a strong Chapter 74 report contains

  • A complete record review — the expert works from the full chart, not excerpts chosen to support a theory;
  • A standard-of-care opinion tied to the specific illness, injury, or condition, and to the circumstances at the time of treatment;
  • A breach analysis identifying precisely how the care rendered departed from that standard;
  • A causation opinion with the chain spelled out — on a challenge, the court asks whether the report represents an objective good-faith effort to cover each required element (§74.351(l)), and a conclusory sentence on causation is the classic deficiency;
  • A CV that proves the qualifications — the CV served with the report should itself evidence the §74.401 criteria: active practice and relevant certification or experience.

Where Texas cases fail

  • Staggered deadlines missed — one 120-day clock tracked in a case where several defendants answered on different dates;
  • Conclusory causation — an opinion that asserts the link between breach and injury without explaining it;
  • The wrong expert — not actively practicing, or experienced with a different condition than the one at issue;
  • A cure that doesn't cure — the single 30-day extension spent on a report that repeats the deficiency;
  • The report treated as a formality — the case is lost at the threshold, before discovery on the merits ever opens.

Defense practices face the mirror-image problem on a shorter fuse: evaluating a served report, and lining up a qualified physician to attack or answer it, inside the 21-day objection window.

Can the §74.351 report expert continue as the testifying expert?

Yes — firms commonly retain the report's author and later designate them as the testifying expert, which preserves continuity from the Chapter 74 report through deposition and trial. The report itself remains inadmissible either way.

Is the report expert the same as a testifying expert?

Not exactly. The §74.351 expert's job is the threshold report — a fair summary of standard of care, breach, and causation served early, while discovery is stayed. A testifying expert is designated later and defends those opinions under cross-examination.
